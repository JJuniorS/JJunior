## So what is the artificial intelligence bubble, anyway?

Over the past three years, Artificial Intelligence stopped being something distant and started to become part of our everyday lives. Some people use AI only to ask questions; others already use it to make decisions or even as a kind of “moral compass,” asking whether a given action was right or wrong.

But there is also a much deeper use: **AI has started to become part of work**.

Today, we developers are using AI for practically everything: reviewing a Pull Request, generating tests for a feature, explaining legacy code, finding a bug, and above all, writing the code itself.

And that is not necessarily a bad thing. Quite the opposite. The productivity these tools unlock is impressive.

The problem begins when we stop seeing AI merely as a tool and start depending on it to carry out tasks we used to do on our own.

That is where the question that made me start thinking about the current “AI bubble” comes in:

> **Who is paying the bill?**

We pay **US$ 20 a month** for tools like Cursor or Claude and get access to models that can generate code, analyze files, write text, and solve extremely complex problems.

But are those US$ 20 and enterprise plans really enough to pay for all of that infrastructure?

Behind every prompt there are GPUs, data centers, electricity, storage, networks, researchers, engineers, and an absurd amount of investment.

If the end user pays relatively little, but the cost of keeping that whole structure running is enormous, **someone has to be covering the gap**.

And that is exactly where my discussion of the Artificial Intelligence bubble begins.

## Who is paying the bill?

There are Business and Enterprise plans, usage-based APIs, corporate contracts, and above all technology companies investing billions in their own infrastructure to ship AI in their products.

[OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/announcing-the-stargate-project/) announced the Stargate project, with **US$ 500 billion** planned in AI infrastructure investment over four years.

[Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/investor/reports/ar25/index.html) invested **US$ 64.6 billion** in property and equipment in fiscal year 2025 alone, including data center infrastructure.

And [NVIDIA](https://investor.nvidia.com/files/doc_financials/2026/q3/13e6981b-95ed-4aac-a602-ebc5865d0590.pdf) reported **US$ 92.6 billion** in Compute & Networking revenue in the first nine months of fiscal year 2026, driven mainly by demand for accelerated computing and AI.

We are talking about an industry that moves **hundreds of billions of dollars**.

And that completely changes the question.

> Will revenue from consumers, companies, and APIs be enough to justify hundreds of billions of dollars in investment?

## What if the math doesn't add up

Here is the main reason I wanted to write about this topic.

I see many developers saying that the moment the bubble bursts and AI becomes economically unviable, we will simply go backwards: go back to developing without AI, reviewing code and features with no help from Artificial Intelligence.

And this is where my take on all of this comes in.

Personally, **I am not that worried about the possibility of a bubble bursting**.

I recently discovered that it is already possible to **run LLMs directly on our own machines**. Obviously, that still requires relatively expensive hardware, mainly because the quality and size of the model we can run locally are directly tied to how much memory is available.

And that market is evolving quickly.

We are already seeing hardware launched with that goal in mind, such as Macs with large amounts of memory and mini PCs based on platforms like Ryzen AI Max, reaching configurations with **96 GB of memory**.

That already makes it possible to run a local LLM with enough capacity to review, write, and analyze code quite competently.

Today we are talking about machines that can cost between **R$ 20,000 and R$ 30,000**. Personally, I do not consider that a better alternative than paying monthly for a cloud AI tool.

But that is not the point.

What I want to show is that, even in the worst possible scenario — where the cost of cloud AI becomes economically unviable — **it does not necessarily mean we go back to where we were before**.

Maybe the technology simply moves somewhere else.

Instead of depending exclusively on large data centers, we may start to see companies investing in machines that can run their own models locally, making AI available directly to their employees.

I do not know if that will be the path.

It is just my view.

But one thing seems clear to me: **AI is advancing too fast in quality, efficiency, and processing power to simply disappear if the current economic model does not work**.

Maybe the bubble exists.

Maybe it bursts.

But I do not believe that would mean the end of Artificial Intelligence.

**Maybe it is just the beginning of a new phase.**
